Choosing between them ​

Both inversions read the same files and return the same kind of result, so the cheapest way to decide is to run both and compare. That is exactly what the shipped demos do. Still, the two have different characters, and it is worth knowing which one your problem suits.

The short version ​

Your situationTry first
blocky, compact targets — a dyke, an ore body, a shallow blockGauss–Newton
gradational structure — a basin, a weathering profile, an alteration haloINR
dense, regular station gridGauss–Newton
sparse or irregular stations, gaps, no-access groundINR
you need an answer in secondsGauss–Newton
depth of the target is the thing you care aboutINR
you want parameters you can explain to a reviewerGauss–Newton

Why they differ ​

Gauss–Newton treats every cell as an independent unknown and then has to argue them back into agreement with a smoothing penalty. The INR never lets them disagree in the first place — a single small network produces all of them, so nearby cells get similar values because nearby inputs give similar outputs.

That has two consequences you can see in the results.

Edges. Gauss–Newton can put a sharp contact exactly where the data demand one. The INR rounds it off, because a smooth function is what it is good at.

Empty ground. Where there are no stations, Gauss–Newton falls back on its smoothing term, which mostly pulls towards the prior. The INR fills the gap by continuing the function it has learned elsewhere — which is usually closer to what a geologist would have drawn.

There is also a bias worth naming: with no term that prices the volume of a cell, Gauss–Newton's sensitivity weighting makes it comfortable putting mass deep and wide, while the INR has no such pull and tends to keep structure where the data put it. In practice that shows up as depth.

What the demos show ​

Two synthetic surveys ship with the package, one built for each case. Both are scored over the survey core, against the true model.

demo1 — two uniform ±200 kg/m³ blocks under a dense 100 m grid. Blocky truth, good coverage, which is the classical setting:

Gauss–Newton0.9910.66
INR1.002810.63

demo2 — a basin whose fill grades continuously with depth, beside a block, under 150 scattered stations with a no-access gap:

true−3.05 Mt52 m
Gauss–Newton0.9530.61−6.34 Mt189 m
INR0.992570.81−2.89 Mt54 m

The second case is the interesting one. Gauss–Newton reaches the target misfit perfectly well — it simply does so with twice the mass it needs, buried more than three times too deep. The INR lands the basin within 2 m of the right depth. And this is not a matter of tuning: across a sweep of stronger smallness, heavier smoothing, depth weighting and no weighting, Gauss–Newton's correlation stays between 0.41 and 0.65 and its basin never comes shallower than 149 m.

Practical advice ​

  1. Run Gauss–Newton first. It is fast, and if your target is blocky and your coverage good, you may not need anything else.

  2. Look at the depths. If the result sits suspiciously deep, or mass is smeared below where you expect it, try the INR.

  3. Run both and compare. Where they agree, you can believe it. Where they disagree, you have found something the data do not actually constrain — which is worth knowing before you put it in a report.

  4. Change the INR's seed and re-run. If the answer moves much, you are asking for more detail than the data support.

Remember that neither method can overcome gravity's fundamental ambiguity. A deeper, denser, larger body and a shallower, lighter, smaller one can produce the same anomaly. Both demos overshoot the true mass, because the data genuinely do not pin it down. Bounds, prior information and geological sense do more for that than the choice of algorithm.
