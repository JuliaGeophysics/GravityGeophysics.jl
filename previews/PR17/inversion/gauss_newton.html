On this page

Gauss–Newton inversion ​

The classical approach, and the one to reach for first. It is fast, predictable, and its behaviour is well understood after thirty years of use on gravity and magnetic data.

julia
run = Invert3D("model.start", "data.xyz", "FwdCtrl",
               "InvCtrl.GN", "C3.dat", "model.prior")

The idea ​

Gravity data never determine a density model on their own — infinitely many models fit any survey equally well. So instead of asking "which model fits the data", we ask for the model that fits the data and is as simple as possible, and we trade the two off:

minimise   ½‖Wd(Gm − d)‖²  +  ½β‖R W (m − mref)‖²

The first term is the data misfit. The second is the regularizer: how far the model has strayed from the prior mref, and how rough it is. β sets the balance. Density bounds are enforced throughout by projection, so the answer always stays geologically plausible.

The settings that matter ​

Bounds ​

Density bounds (kg/m3) : -400 400

Do not leave these wide. They are the cheapest and most physical regularization you have — set them from the rock types you actually expect, and the inversion cannot invent densities that do not exist in your area.

Smallness and smoothing ​

Smallness weight   : 0.04
Smoothing weight x : 1
Smoothing weight y : 1
Smoothing weight z : 1

Smallness keeps the model near the prior; smoothing ties neighbouring cells together. The weights are relative to a core cell, so smallness = 1/L² corresponds roughly to a smoothing length of L core cells.

Raise smoothing if the model is speckled. Raise z on its own if you are getting vertical streaks, which gravity is prone to.

Weighting — do not skip this ​

Sensitivity weighting    : sensitivity
Depth weighting exponent : 2
Depth weighting z0 (m)   : auto

Gravity falls off with distance, so a cell just under a station influences the data far more than a deep one. Left alone, the inversion exploits this and plasters everything into the top layer, because that is the cheapest way to fit the data.

  • sensitivity weighting (Li & Oldenburg 2000) uses the sensitivity matrix itself to compensate. This is the default and usually the right choice.

  • depth weighting (Li & Oldenburg 1998) uses an analytic (z + z₀)^(-β/2) instead.

  • none is available but expect a surface-hugging model.

Beta cooling ​

Initial damping factor lambda : auto
Lambda cooling factor         : 2

β starts large (a smooth, heavily regularized model) and is divided by the cooling factor after each accepted iteration, letting structure in gradually until the target misfit is reached. auto picks the starting value from the ratio of the largest eigenvalues of the data and model terms, which is almost always better than guessing.

If a cooled step overshoots the target misfit, the solver bisects β geometrically to land in [0.9, 1] × target rather than blowing past it — the discrepancy principle.

Under the hood ​

Each iteration solves for a step with projected, Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients over the cells that are off their bounds, then does a backtracking line search. The dense sensitivity matrix is held once in Float32. Maximum CG iterations and CG tolerance control the inner solve; the defaults are rarely worth changing.

Strengths and limits ​

Good at: blocky, compact targets. Dense, regular surveys. Being predictable — the same settings give the same answer, and the parameters mean something you can reason about.

Less good at: smoothly varying structure, which the piecewise-constant cell parameterization represents awkwardly; and sparse, irregular surveys, where it tends to put mass where the stations happen to be. In examples/demo2 — a graded basin under a scattered survey — it recovers twice the mass it needs and buries it more than three times too deep, and that holds across a wide sweep of its regularization settings.

That is the case the neural inversion was added for.
